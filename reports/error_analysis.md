# Error Analysis

This document examines the structure of baseline errors at the default operating point `tau = 0.25`. The analysis works from two data sources: the matched ground-truth and prediction records in `runs/20260426_135344_groupsplit_test/` (full 2,971-frame group-split test set), and twelve example images per error category saved under `reports/error_cases/`. Section "Calibration" at the end of this document is the part F deliverable that asks whether the score is interpretable as a probability; numbers come from `reports/calibration.json`.

## What kinds of errors the baseline produces

At `tau = 0.25` the model produces 13,843 true positives, 2,648 false positives with no ground-truth match, 354 boxes that are localized correctly but assigned the wrong class, and 5,019 missed ground-truth boxes. Misses outnumber false positives by almost 2:1 — the dominant failure mode is under-detection rather than hallucination. This pattern was already established in the data audit (section 5) and is reproduced here so the rest of the document can refer to it without restating the headline numbers.

The 1,485 scenes that contain at least one false positive, the 1,786 scenes that contain at least one missed ground-truth box, and the 2,550 scenes with at least one true positive (one scene can appear in multiple categories) are saved to `reports/error_cases/` with twelve examples each. The 371 empty scenes that the model also returns empty are saved as the true-negative set. Half of each set is the highest-confidence example (most "egregious" cases), half is a random sample for diversity.

## Three categories of errors

The ranking, slice, and per-class data taken together support three distinct error categories rather than one undifferentiated noise term. Each of the following is a mechanism, not just a symptom, and each is visible in the saved error cases.

The first category is **small-object misses**. Pedestrians, bikers, and all traffic-light state classes have recall in the 0.43 to 0.71 range, well below the 0.72 aggregate. The mechanism is the 1920×1200 → 512×512 stretch resize from preprocessing (subdomain S9 in the data audit). Tall narrow objects lose vertical pixels first; small distant objects lose all distinguishing pixels. The score signature in the false-negative directory is consistent with this: when the model does emit a box for a small object, the confidence is low (median around 0.30 for predictions that overlap a missed ground-truth box but fall below the threshold). The model is not failing to learn that these objects exist — it is failing to localize them confidently enough to clear the threshold. Re-tuning `tau` cannot recover these errors because for many of them the model emits no box at all even at `tau = 0.001`.

The second category is **annotation-overlap confusion in the trafficLight family**. The 354 wrong-class matches at IoU >= 0.5 are dominated by `trafficLight-Red → trafficLight` (32 cases) and `trafficLight → trafficLight-Green` (17 cases), with `trafficLight-Red → trafficLight-RedLeft` (12) as a third pattern. The mechanism here is annotation noise documented in section 1.5 of the data audit: the bare `trafficLight` class is used by annotators when the state cannot be determined from the image, so it semantically overlaps with all the colored subclasses. The model is not making an error — it is reproducing inconsistent labels. This category will not improve with more training; it is an irreducible upper bound set by the dataset's class taxonomy. The false-positive examples in the saved set show several cases where a confidently-detected `trafficLight` box overlaps a ground-truth `trafficLight-Red`, which inflates the wrong-class count without representing a real model failure.

The third category is **dense-scene precision loss**, visible in the 1,758 false positives on the `car` class. The mechanism is two-sided. About half of these are duplicate-bounding-box artifacts — the dataset README warns that overlapping ground-truth boxes on the same vehicle are present and were not cleaned, so a single correctly-detected vehicle generates one true positive and one false positive when the GT contains a duplicate. The other half are real model errors in cluttered traffic scenes where vehicles are partly occluded by other vehicles; the model emits speculative boxes for visible fragments. The false-positive directory shows both patterns side by side: some images have two near-identical predicted boxes overlapping one ground truth, others show extra boxes in cluttered traffic with no ground-truth analogue. Distinguishing the two requires looking at the overlay images, which is why they are saved.

## Where errors sit relative to the decision boundary

Errors split into "boundary" errors that move with the threshold and "structural" errors that do not. The boundary errors are most of the false positives in the `tau` range 0.25 to 0.40: precision rises from 0.82 to 0.89 across that range with a recall cost of 0.07 (from 0.72 to 0.64), which means the marginal false positives between 0.25 and 0.40 are not concentrated on a fixed set of scenes but distributed across many. Lowering `tau` to 0.20 trades back a small precision drop for very little recall gain, suggesting the curve is locally flat in that region — there is no obvious natural threshold the model is trying to pick out.

The structural errors are the misses on small objects and the trafficLight class confusion. Both are insensitive to `tau` because the model either does not emit a box at all (misses) or emits a confident box with the wrong class (the `trafficLight` confusions are typically at confidence 0.5 to 0.8). Threshold movement does not help either case.

## Calibration of the score

The reliability diagram in `reports/figures/reliability_diagram.png` and the numerical values in `reports/calibration.json` show that the model is systematically under-confident in the operationally relevant confidence range. Aggregated ECE depends strongly on whether low-confidence rejections are included in the sample. For the matched-only sample (predictions with `conf >= 0.25`, the operationally visible range), ECE is around 0.20 and every populated bin shows accuracy above mean confidence by 10 to 30 points. A box predicted at confidence 0.55 is correct 86% of the time on this dataset.

This means the score cannot be used directly as a probability. Treating it as `P(correct | s)` would understate the model's reliability everywhere it matters operationally. The score is monotonic with correctness — higher score means higher correctness rate, in every populated bin — so it is a usable ranking signal for the operating-point choice in `operating_point.md`. But any downstream system that needs a calibrated probability (a probabilistic fusion stage, a cost-sensitive thresholder that compares model confidence against a fixed risk budget) needs an explicit calibration step, which the baseline does not provide.

The systematic direction of the miscalibration (under-confident, not over-confident) is the safer of the two failure modes for a driving-context detector — emitted detections are more likely to be correct than the score suggests. But it does mean that the threshold-based operating-point arithmetic in `operating_point.md` is using `s` as an operational score rather than as a probability, and any reader who interprets `tau = 0.30` as "I want at least 30% chance the box is correct" will be wrong. The right reading is "I want the score to be at least 0.30," and the empirical accuracy at that score level is documented in the reliability diagram.

The shift behavior of calibration is recorded in section 5 of `stress_test.md`: under blur shift the under-confidence pattern is preserved in the bins that survive, and aggregate ECE drops because the high-confidence bins empty out. This means the calibration story does not change qualitatively under the shifts tested in seminar 3 — the model's score retains its monotonic ranking property and remains under-confident in actionable bins, even when the absolute detection count collapses.
